# Industry Research

Build understanding that changes decisions, not a warehouse of summaries. Preserve useful evidence in the research backend while keeping the reader-facing report short, coherent, and current.

## Choose the route

Infer the route from the request and existing files. Ask only when an unanswered choice would materially change the result.

1. **New industry report** — establish boundaries, answer high-value questions, and create one authoritative report.
2. **Focused question** — investigate one market, product, strategy, or causal question without filling an industry template.
3. **Company/product brief** — place a company, portfolio, announced pipeline, strategy, and capabilities inside its industry context.
4. **Material intake** — evaluate new URLs, files, notes, or claims and update an existing research system.
5. **Report compression** — remove repetition, merge weak sections, simplify hierarchy, and preserve only decision-relevant content.

Select research intensity before working: **quick** for bounded lookups or material triage, **standard** for multi-source synthesis and most company/industry work, and **deep** only for high-impact, disputed, or strategically costly questions. For standard or deep work, read [references/evidence-and-maintenance.md](references/evidence-and-maintenance.md). Read [references/deliverables.md](references/deliverables.md) when selecting or editing an output structure.

## Define the research contract

Before broad research, state or infer:

- the decision or capability the report should improve;
- audience and expected reading depth;
- geography, time period, channel, product boundary, and business-model boundary;
- deliverable route and authoritative destination;
- known unknowns that could change the conclusion.

Do not turn every possible analytical dimension into a chapter. Build a compact internal claim map. Give each candidate claim a stable label, the decision it affects, its current evidence state, and what would change it. Prioritize claims by decision impact, uncertainty, and cost of being wrong—not by how easy they are to search. A focused request may need only a few claims.

## Inspect before searching

When files or an existing report are available:

1. Read the authoritative report, project index, and evidence ledger first.
2. Locate existing conclusions, definitions, gaps, and superseded material.
3. Search locally before creating a new file or section.
4. Treat historical drafts as evidence of evolution, not as equal user-facing outputs.

Maintain one clearly named authoritative report. Backend files may grow; the main report must earn every paragraph.

## Research by claim, not by source

For each important claim, define what evidence would confirm, limit, conflict with, or overturn it. Select sources according to the claim:

- market size and behavior: official statistics, platform disclosures, well-defined datasets;
- company facts and pipeline: company site, filings, official announcements, store pages, verified interviews;
- financial and operating performance: audited filings, earnings releases, regulator records;
- policy and platform rules: current first-party policy or regulator text;
- product behavior: direct observation, versioned store/build evidence, then credible analysis;
- user needs and causal effects: primary research, experiments, representative surveys, or clearly bounded qualitative evidence.

Search current information whenever facts may have changed. Prefer primary sources, then independent high-quality secondary sources. Use secondary articles to discover originals, not to multiply one underlying source into apparent consensus. Every follow-up search should close a named gap, test an alternative explanation, or resolve a conflict.

Record event date separately from publication date. Preserve exact scope, unit, definition, sample, and method for important numbers. Explain conflicts through boundary, period, methodology, or incentives rather than averaging incompatible figures.

## Separate epistemic layers

Never blend these into one voice:

- **Fact** — directly observed or documented.
- **Source claim** — an interpretation made by a named source.
- **Research inference** — a conclusion derived from stated evidence.
- **Hypothesis** — plausible but awaiting verification.

Use confident report language only to the level supported. Admit a claim as a report conclusion only when its evidence matches its importance. Otherwise label it as disputed, single-source, or unresolved and limit the conclusion. A supported partial report is better than false completeness.

## Close gaps without over-researching

After the first evidence pass:

1. identify which open gaps could still change a conclusion or action;
2. search those gaps using evidence-derived terms and likely primary sources;
3. test the strongest alternative explanation and relevant counterexample;
4. stop when new retrieval is unlikely to change a decision, boundary, or confidence level.

Do not use fixed source counts as a substitute for judgment. One authoritative source may settle a documented fact; causal, comparative, or contested claims usually need independent origins or an explicit limitation. For deep or high-stakes work, retain a compact research run with claim, source, gap, conflict, and stop-reason records. Do not create those artifacts for ordinary quick work.

## Convert information into a decision chain

Organize analysis around:

`evidence → mechanism → boundary → implication → action or monitoring signal`

For recommendations, add the affected actor, prerequisites, expected value, cost/risk, minimum validation, and stop condition. Distinguish a growing market from an attractive profit pool and from an opportunity accessible to the specific team.

For company research, distinguish:

- proven released products;
- officially announced or test-stage products;
- credible but unconfirmed reports;
- inference about capabilities or strategy.

Never present rumors, job postings, trademarks, or recruitment language as a confirmed release pipeline.

## Process every new material

Assign one primary route:

1. **Discard** — duplicate, off-scope, promotional, unsupported, or too costly to verify.
2. **Lead only** — useful pointer whose original evidence still needs retrieval.
3. **Strengthen backend evidence** — supports an existing conclusion but changes no reader decision.
4. **Integrate and revise** — changes a conclusion, boundary, framework, action, or priority; replace or compress existing text.
5. **New-section candidate** — answers a genuinely new high-value decision question that cannot fit the current structure.

Default to integration, replacement, or deletion—not append-only growth. Add a section only when it passes all gates in the maintenance reference.

After an intake, report briefly:

- usefulness and evidence quality;
- chosen route and reason;
- exact section or backend asset affected;
- conclusion or action changed;
- net effect on main-report length.

## Write for learning and use

Lead with conclusions and decision implications. Use plain professional language. Delete throat-clearing, source-by-source retelling, duplicated definitions, ornamental examples, and unsupported precision.

Explain only terms necessary to understand the argument. At first use, prefer a compact callout:

> **Term — concise definition.** Closely related concept or common confusion, only if it prevents a real misunderstanding.

Keep vivid practitioner remarks only when they add intuition. Place them after the formal conclusion as a labeled note, not as the argument itself.

Use tables only for exact comparisons, mappings, or decision criteria. Do not create tables that merely repeat prose.

## Preserve scope and authorization

Reading, researching, and drafting do not authorize writes to external documents. Edit local or external reports only when the user requested the change. Preserve unrelated material and existing user work. If the target is ambiguous, deliver a proposed insertion or revision rather than mutating multiple documents.

## Quality gate

Before delivery, apply three gates.

**Truth gate**

- the report answers the stated decision questions;
- major claims have nearby citations or explicit evidence status;
- sources are independent at the data-generating origin when corroboration matters;
- time, geography, units, samples, and definitions are comparable;
- facts, source claims, inference, and hypotheses remain distinguishable;
- counter-evidence and material uncertainty are visible;
- conclusions include boundaries and do not overgeneralize from winners or anecdotes;

**Decision gate**

- each recommendation has a mechanism and validation path;
- the report distinguishes market growth, profit-pool attractiveness, and team-accessible opportunity;
- the strongest alternative explanation has been considered;

**Compression gate**

- the main report has no repeated point or section that costs more attention than it returns;
- examples prove or clarify a point rather than decorate it;
- the final handoff names what changed and what remains uncertain.

If evidence is insufficient, deliver the supported partial conclusion, open gaps, and highest-value next verification step. Do not manufacture completeness.
